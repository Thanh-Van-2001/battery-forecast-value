\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

We asked whether a more accurate load forecast lowers a building's bill when it is used to schedule a battery, in a pre-specified study of \res{nKept} buildings. Under a demand charge, the ranking of five standard forecasters by MAE did not agree with their ranking by saving. The sign of the error explains why: under-forecasts at charging hours set new monthly peaks while over-forecasts only keep charge in reserve, so the least accurate, peak-conservative forecaster captured the largest share of the attainable saving, and an asymmetric score agreed, weakly, with value where the MAE did not. How the schedule was executed mattered more than the forecaster, since open-loop schedules raised the bill in most buildings while a simple real-time guard made them save. Without a demand charge, accuracy did not matter at all. Exploratory analyses added after the study point the same way: the most accurate forecaster, a current foundation model, raised the bill when its median was executed open loop, while its own 0.9 quantile saved the most, and value rose with the quantile level as accuracy fell. With a headroom margin in the schedule, accuracy became weakly informative, so the null result for the MAE belongs to the plain day-ahead program; the asymmetric score remained the better guide under every controller tested. Forecasts for building storage should be judged by asymmetric, decision-relevant scores under the controller that will use them.
